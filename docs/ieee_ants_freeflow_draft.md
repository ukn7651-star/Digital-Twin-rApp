# How Much Link-Level Realism Does a Network Digital Twin Need? An Open-Source Multi-Cell 5G Twin Coupling Ray Tracing with a Real Protocol Stack

**Abstract** — Network digital twins promise to let operators and researchers evaluate radio access network (RAN) algorithms in software before touching a live network, but existing open platforms force a choice between fidelity and scale: full-protocol-stack emulators reproduce one link faithfully, while system-level simulators cover whole networks with abstracted physical layers. We present a hybrid-fidelity, fully open-source digital twin that refuses this choice. Site-specific channels for every cell-to-UE link in a multi-cell, multi-UE network are computed with ray tracing (NVIDIA Sionna RT) over real building geometry (OpenStreetMap); a fast analytical layer produces network-wide per-UE and per-cell downlink throughput in seconds using standard link-abstraction techniques anchored to measurements of a real 5G-NR physical layer (OpenAirInterface's `nr_dlsim`); and a ground-truth layer runs selected links through a live OpenAirInterface gNB–UE stack over the exact ray-traced channel, with each link's computed inter-cell interference folded into the emulated noise floor so that single-link runs retain their multi-cell context. Because all fidelity levels share one scene, one network, and one channel, the platform can answer a question that is usually only debated qualitatively: how much link-level realism does a network digital twin actually need? We compare three throughput engines — an idealized link-level model (Sionna SYS), a real-stack-measured link abstraction, and the full stack in the loop — on identical scenarios, and report where and by how much they diverge. The entire platform runs on one commodity Linux server with a single GPU, uses only open-source software and open data, and is designed to close the loop: its KPIs feed O-RAN rApps whose decisions act back on the network, enabling pre-deployment training and validation of RAN automation — a capability that today exists only in proprietary toolchains.

**Index terms** — digital twin, 5G NR, OpenAirInterface, Sionna RT, ray tracing, O-RAN, rApp, link abstraction.

## I. Introduction

The cost of experimenting on real cellular networks — licenses, hardware, drive tests, the risk of degrading live service — has made the digital twin one of the most discussed tools in RAN research and standardization. 3GPP and the O-RAN ALLIANCE both treat network digital twins (NDTs) as key enablers, in particular as environments in which AI/ML-driven network automation applications (xApps and rApps in O-RAN terms) can be trained and validated before deployment.

But a digital twin is only as useful as the fidelity of the data it produces, and here the open tooling landscape splits into two camps that do not meet. On one side, system-level simulators — ns-3/5G-LENA, Simu5G, and proprietary NDTs — scale to many cells and users but abstract the physical layer into lookup tables and formulas; a scheduler in such a simulator never runs real LDPC decoding, never experiences a real HARQ round trip. On the other side, protocol-stack emulators built on OpenAirInterface (OAI) execute the genuine 5G-NR stack, bit for bit, but published integrations with realistic propagation cover a single gNB–UE link. The Open Wireless Digital Twin (OWDT) of Iye et al., the closest prior work to ours, convolves ray-traced channel impulse responses into OAI's baseband in real time along a vehicle trajectory — and its authors state plainly that the platform is limited to one gNB, one UE, SISO, with multi-node operation left to future work requiring FPGA offload.

The position of this paper is that the dichotomy is false, because different questions need different fidelity at different places in the network. Whether UE 17 attaches to cell A or cell B is a geometry-and-power question; whether its 6.2 dB SINR yields MCS 11 or MCS 13 with 10% BLER is a question about a real receiver. We therefore build the twin in two coupled layers over one shared physics substrate: a fast analytical layer that covers every UE and every cell, and a real-stack layer that covers sampled links exactly — with a calibration path from the latter to the former, and a designed interface (interference folded into the noise floor) that lets the single-link real stack inherit its multi-cell context.

Our contributions:

1. **An open-source, multi-cell, multi-UE digital twin architecture** combining OSM geometry, Sionna RT ray tracing, an analytical multi-cell interference layer, and OpenAirInterface as the link-realism engine — occupying exactly the operating point (network scale × real stack) that prior open platforms do not.
2. **A real-stack-anchored link abstraction.** The fast layer's SINR→MCS mapping is measured from OAI's PHY simulator (`nr_dlsim`: real LDPC, real rate matching) rather than idealized tables, and is driven by per-resource-element SINRs with channel-dependent MRT beamforming, compressed via EESM so that the frequency selectivity computed by the ray tracer survives into throughput. A single β-scale knob makes the abstraction calibratable against the full stack.
3. **A multi-cell-aware single-link emulation method.** For each UE, the exact ray-traced taps of its serving link are injected into a live OAI rfsimulator link (a small patch to OAI's channel module, verified in operation), and the UE's computed inter-cell interference plus thermal noise is applied as the emulated noise floor — so per-link ground truth reflects the interference environment of the full network.
4. **A quantified fidelity comparison.** Because all engines share one scene and one channel, we report per-UE throughput divergence between (a) an idealized link-level model (Sionna SYS), (b) the real-stack-measured abstraction, and (c) the full stack in the loop — evidence toward the question in the title.
5. **A path to closed-loop rApp validation**, which we argue is the purpose that should shape twin design, and which no open-source platform currently provides.

## II. Related work

**Single-link, full-stack emulation.** OWDT (Iye et al.) integrates Sionna RT with OAI on SDR hardware and a commercial UE module, replaying time-evolving CIRs along SUMO vehicle trajectories and monitoring KPIs through FlexRIC. Its real-time convolution budget caps the channel at 28 taps, and it is explicitly single-gNB, single-UE, SISO, uncalibrated. Our work is complementary rather than competing: we trade their mobility and over-the-air hardware for network scale, software reproducibility, and calibration, and we adopt virtual-time simulation (OAI's vrtsim) in our roadmap precisely to escape the real-time ceiling they document. Within the OAI project itself, a ray-tracing channel emulator with REST scene control has recently appeared alongside vrtsim; our architecture is designed to adopt these as the injection backend as they mature — our contribution is the network layer above the injection, not the injection itself.

**Waveform-level multi-node emulators.** Colosseum convolves baseband among many nodes on an FPGA cluster (4-tap channels); NVIDIA's Aerial Omniverse Digital Twin, recently released openly, simulates city-scale networks with GPU-accelerated physics but with NVIDIA's own RAN/UE simulators rather than a deployable open stack, on NVIDIA-specific infrastructure. Both occupy the "true waveform combining" point that we deliberately do not chase (Section VI): for throughput prediction with standard receivers, interference-as-noise is the physically appropriate model, and it is the model every system-level tool uses.

**System-level simulators with ray tracing.** Integrations of Sionna RT with ns-3/5G-LENA bring deterministic channels to network simulation but keep abstracted PHY/MAC. Our fast layer is philosophically similar — we use the same link-abstraction methodology — with the difference that our abstraction is measured from, and calibratable against, a real stack that lives inside the same platform.

**Commercial NDTs and the rApp loop.** Rimedo Labs' Network Digital Twin, coupled to the commercial VIAVI emulator, trains and validates O-RAN traffic-steering rApps before deployment; their published proof-of-concept reports ~13% throughput gain at 17 cells / 80 emulated UEs. This closed loop — twin KPIs → rApp decision → network reaction → new KPIs — is precisely the industrial use case for twins, and it is available today only in proprietary form. Our platform is architected to reproduce this loop with open components, and additionally to publish the number commercial vendors do not: how far the twin's predicted rApp gain deviates from the stack-measured gain.

## III. System architecture

The platform is a pipeline with two consumers of one physics substrate.

**Geometry.** A WGS84 bounding box drives a live OpenStreetMap (Overpass) query for building footprints, which are extruded to 3D and compiled into a Mitsuba scene with ITU material properties. In our running example (a ~400×400 m area of central Berlin) this yields 2,623 buildings.

**Network data.** Cells and UEs are supplied through a swappable data-source interface. A seeded random generator (N sites × 3 sectors, uniform UE drops) supports controlled experiments; a CSV source accepts real site data in WGS84 lat/lon (the format of open databases such as OpenCelliD), projects it into the scene frame, and auto-sectorizes sites — so the same pipeline runs synthetic scenarios and twins of actual deployments.

**Channel.** Sionna RT places one transmitter per cell (3GPP sector antenna pattern, configurable planar array, downtilt) and one receiver per UE, and computes the channel frequency response for every cell→UE link over a representative OFDM resource grid — in the running example, a tensor of shape [30 UEs × 1 UE-antenna × 9 cells × 4 BS-antennas × 12 symbols × 128 subcarriers]. This CFR is the single source of physical truth for everything downstream.

**Fast layer (all UEs, all cells, seconds).** Each UE associates to the strongest cell by wideband received power. Its SINR is computed per resource element: the serving signal uses the true coherent single-stream beamforming gain σ²max(Hk) — the matched-filter (MRT) gain, which is the optimal precoder when a cell serves one UE at a time under airtime sharing, and which unlike a flat antenna-count bonus depends on the actual ray-traced channel. Interference from other cells enters as noise, as the expected power of a random beam (‖Hk‖²F/Ntx), scaled by a configurable neighbor load factor (full-buffer = 1.0 is a worst case that systematically overestimates interference in real networks). The per-RE SINR vector is compressed to an AWGN-equivalent effective SINR by EESM with per-modulation-order β parameters, and mapped through the **OAI-measured link curve**: an offline sweep of `nr_dlsim` across SNR × MCS records the lowest SNR at which each MCS meets the 10% first-transmission BLER target, giving the real stack's link-adaptation staircase. A single global β-scale is the calibration knob. Scheduling is equal airtime per cell — which on a static full-buffer snapshot is not an approximation but the provable fixed point of proportional fairness. Outputs are per-UE and per-cell throughput (CSV/JSON) plus the exported channel for the ground-truth layer.

**Ground-truth layer (sampled links, real stack).** For a selected UE, the serving link's CFR is converted to time-domain taps (IFFT, strongest causal taps, unit-energy normalization with link gain carried separately) and injected into a live OAI gNB↔UE rfsimulator link through a small patch to OAI's channel module; we have verified in the gNB log that the ray-traced taps are loaded and the link runs MCS/BLER/HARQ over them. The key architectural device is the noise floor: the twin computes, per UE, thermal noise plus the aggregate inter-cell interference power from the fast layer, and applies that value as the emulated channel's noise power (OAI exposes this at runtime through its telnet interface). The single-link emulation thereby "feels" the multi-cell network it belongs to. With the full stack (gNB + open-source 5G core) and multiple UE processes in network namespaces — each with its own per-client channel model, a stock OAI capability — real per-UE goodput is measured with iperf3 through the UE's tunnel interface, with the real MAC scheduler arbitrating PRBs among UEs. KPIs (MCS, BLER, SNR, goodput) are parsed from stack logs today, and via FlexRIC/E2SM-KPM in the O-RAN-aligned configuration.

## IV. Experimental design: three engines, one network

The platform deliberately contains three interchangeable throughput engines over the same scene, network, and CFR:

- **E1 — idealized link-level model**: the Sionna SYS chain (RZF precoded channel, LMMSE post-equalization SINR, PHY-abstraction BLER tables, inner-loop link adaptation). This represents the state of practice in GPU-native system simulation.
- **E2 — real-stack-measured abstraction**: the fast layer of Section III (per-RE MRT SINR, EESM, `nr_dlsim`-measured curve).
- **E3 — full stack in the loop**: OAI gNB↔UE over the exact injected channel with calibrated noise floor; iperf3 goodput.

Divergence between E1 and E2 isolates the effect of replacing idealized receiver tables with a real receiver's measured staircase. Divergence between E2 and E3 isolates everything a formula cannot capture: HARQ dynamics, scheduler behavior, protocol overheads, decoder implementation. The per-UE throughput divergence CDFs across these pairs are, to our knowledge, the first such quantification on a shared open platform — and they double as the calibration data for E2's β knob.

The OAI-measured link curve spans MCS 0–28, with e.g. MCS 0 sustainable at −2.6 dB and the curve's staircase measured at RB=106. Exact-tap injection into the live rfsimulator is verified operational (taps and delays loaded into the channel, link running HARQ over them), with one documented calibration caveat: OAI's closed-loop uplink power control compensates injected path loss — correct 5G behavior — so the downlink at the UE is the clean observable for absolute level calibration.

## VI. Limitations

We state these explicitly because a twin's trustworthiness depends on knowing what it does not model. (1) Inter-cell interference is modeled as noise; cells' waveforms are never physically combined. For throughput with standard (non-interference-cancelling) receivers this matches what the UE experiences, and it is the model of every system-level tool; waveform-level combining — needed for CoMP/ICIC or receiver research — requires emulators of the Colosseum/AODT class and is out of scope. (2) The ground-truth layer scales to a handful-to-a-dozen concurrent real UEs per server; the two-layer design makes this a sampling constraint rather than a coverage constraint. (3) The present snapshot is static — no mobility or Doppler; for the throughput question this is a scope boundary rather than an error source, and the roadmap adds mobility through OAI's virtual-time simulator where OWDT's real-time tap ceiling does not apply. (4) MIMO in the loop is limited (~2×2 in rfsimulator); massive-MIMO gains remain analytic (MRT) in the fast layer. (5) All cells currently share one carrier (worst-case co-channel interference). (6) The engine is downlink-only. (7) Validation is stack-versus-model, not model-versus-field; calibration against operator measurements is the single most valuable extension.

## VII. Roadmap: closing the loop

The reason to build a twin at this fidelity is what sits on top. The platform's KPIs feed an O-RAN rApp — our first target is traffic steering by cell-offset adjustment — which is trained on fast-layer synthetic scenarios (cheap, unlimited) and then validated closed-loop against the ground-truth layer: the rApp's decisions are applied to the emulated network, the stack reacts, and we measure whether the gain the twin predicted is the gain the stack delivers. Handover machinery for this exists in OAI today (F1/N2, externally triggered — which in an rApp-driven architecture is the intended control path). The deliverable is both the open-source loop itself, whose commercial analogue is proprietary, and the twin-to-stack fidelity gap on a control task — the number that ultimately answers whether digital twins can be trusted to train the algorithms that will run real networks.

## VIII. Conclusion

We presented an open-source digital twin that pairs network-scale analytical modeling with real-protocol-stack ground truth over shared ray-traced physics, on commodity hardware. It improves on idealized system simulation by anchoring every throughput number to a measured real receiver; it improves on single-link stack emulation by preserving the multi-cell, multi-UE context that network questions require; and it is built to close the loop with O-RAN automation applications. The quantitative comparison it enables — model versus abstraction versus stack — replaces an article of faith in twin design with a measurement.
